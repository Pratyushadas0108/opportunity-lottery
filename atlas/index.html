Progression Pipeline Continuity Analysis

Testing whether strength at one transition stage translates into strength at the next stage across 36 macro administrative entities.

Link 1: School Quality $\leftrightarrow$ Higher Education

r = +0.512 (Moderate-to-Strong)

States with strong public school infrastructure, low dropout rates, and high secondary transition systematically co-occur with higher tertiary enrolment (e.g., Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Chandigarh).

Link 2: Higher Education $\leftrightarrow$ Youth Jobs

r = +0.058 (No Linear Link)

Tertiary expansion shows no clear linear correlation with immediate youth employment dynamism in cross-sectional data. Educated youth often experience longer search queues for formal jobs rather than accepting informal work.

The Analyst's Five Core Empirical Insights

1. A 25-point gap separates the top and bottom of the opportunity index

Across 36 states and UTs, the top five average 67.8 and the bottom five 42.5. Among the 28 full states, Sikkim ranks first, with Tamil Nadu as the highest-ranked large state, and Bihar is last at 35.5. The ranking holds under three alternative weighting scenarios (four weighting schemes in total, Spearman $ ho \ge 0.90$).

Caveat: This is a relative index built from data of different years, so it ranks states against each other and says nothing about absolute levels.

2. The gender gap appears at work, not in school

Girls drop out of secondary school less than boys (9.6% vs 13.3%, UDISE+ AY 2024–25) and have a higher college enrolment ratio (31.2 vs 28.9, AISHE AY 2023–24). Yet male labour force participation is 79.1% against 40.0% for women, a 39.1 percentage point gap (PLFS CY 2025).

Caveat: Three different years and sources, and participation covers all women aged 15+, not only graduates.

3. Better school and college access go together, but neither lines up with youth job outcomes

School and higher-education scores are moderately correlated (r = 0.51), while higher-education access and youth job scores show almost no relationship (r = 0.06). Kerala ranks near the top for schools and colleges but 27th on jobs, with youth unemployment of about 20.8%. Gujarat is first on jobs with 2.5% youth unemployment, yet 21st in college enrolment.

Caveat: Only 36 observations, and half the jobs index is based on unemployment. Educated youth searching for better jobs and out-migration are possible explanations that this data cannot test.

4. Two states can have the same score and need different help

Clusters 3 and 4 have nearly identical composite scores (48.0 and 47.1), but different shapes. The first (mostly northeastern and hill states) is relatively strong on gender (55.2) and weak on schools (50.6). The second (including Bihar, UP, and Rajasthan) has better schools (62.0) but the weakest higher education (36.3) and gender scores (39.4). Meghalaya shows the same pattern: a top-tier gender score (64.8) alongside school electrification of only 28.1%.

Caveat: Clusters are statistical groupings from k-means with k=4 and are not official categories.

5. Higher literacy and urbanization do not guarantee higher female labour participation

Delhi is 97.5% urban with female literacy of 84.4%, yet it has the lowest female labour force participation in the country at 13.1%. Among full states, Haryana (23.8%) and Bihar (24.7%) are lowest.

Caveat: This describes a pattern in survey data, and the reasons (job availability, safety, social norms, how work is measured) are outside what this project tested.

Targeted Policy Recommendations

Recommendation 1: Public School Infrastructure Funding

Target States (5 lowest School Access scores): Meghalaya (15.74), Arunachal Pradesh (38.70), Bihar (48.27), Manipur (50.18), Mizoram (50.84).

Meghalaya’s electrification figure (28.1%) shows basic facilities are the binding constraint. Check district-level data before allocating money, since state averages hide significant local variation.

Recommendation 2: College Capacity and Faculty Funding

Target States (5 lowest Higher Ed Access scores): Bihar (28.26), Jharkhand (29.21), D&NH and D&D (31.58), Ladakh (32.89), Andaman & Nicobar (33.78).

Focus college capacity and faculty funding on Bihar and Jharkhand first (Bihar has 61.3 students per teacher and only 8.07 colleges per lakh population). Small UTs on the same list probably need different solutions, such as scholarships to study elsewhere or distance learning options, because they cannot support many standalone colleges.

Recommendation 3: Female Workforce Transition Support

Target States (5 lowest Female LFPR): Delhi (13.1%), Lakshadweep (21.9%), Haryana (23.8%), Bihar (24.7%), Goa (27.4%).

Pair skilling programs for women with job-access support (safe transit, childcare). Female literacy is already high in several of them, so the data suggests the barrier lies after education. Start with a pilot, track employment outcomes, and test local reasons before scaling.
